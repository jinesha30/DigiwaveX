EXPERIMENT 1: To study theory of digital line coding and decoding.
In digital transmission, line codes shape baseband pulses to eliminate DC bias, prevent baseline wander in transformers, and guarantee mid-bit clock transitions for PLL bit synchronizers.
1. Level Slicing & Thresholds
The receiver comparator compares the incoming waveform against decision thresholds (+0.5V, 0V, -0.5V). For AMI, marks are detected when $|V| > 0.25V$.
2. Mid-Bit Clock Sampling
At the 10 KHz clock rising edge ($T_b/2 = 50\,\mu\text{s}$), the eye is wide open. Sampling at mid-bit provides maximum noise margin and prevents intersymbol interference.
3. Error Detection (Code Violations)
In AMI, if two consecutive 1s occur with identical polarity, an AMI Code Violation (CV) is triggered instantly, providing built-in transmission error logging.

EXPERIMENT 1: To study theory of digital line coding and decoding

Objective: To generate digital line coded waveforms for a given 8-bit word and reconstruct the original binary word using on-board line decoders.

Procedure:
  1. Connect 230V AC, 50 Hz power cord to the trainer and turn ON the power rocker switch.
  2. Set an 8-bit test word (e.g., 10110010) using the 8 toggle switches D7 to D0 on the Data Generator block.
  3. Connect patch cord from DATA OUT (TX) to DATA IN of Line Encoder.
  4. Connect patch cord from CLK OUT (10 KHz) to CLK IN of Line Encoder.
  5. Patch SIG OUT of Line Encoder to SCOPE CH2 IN to observe the encoded waveform.
  6. Patch SIG OUT of Line Encoder to DEC IN of Line Decoder to observe reconstructed 8-bit data LEDs.
  7. Verify that the recovered 8-bit LEDs match the transmitted word D7..D0 with zero bit errors.

EXPERIMENT 2: To observe spectrum of different line codes on Spectrum Analyzer

Objective: To observe the Power Spectral Density (PSD) and spectral characteristics of different line codes using a Spectrum Analyzer.

Procedure:
  1. Connect patch cord from SIG OUT of the Line Encoder to SPECTRUM IN of the Spectrum Analyzer.
  2. Set the frequency span to 40 kHz (center frequency 20 kHz).
  3. Select Unipolar NRZ: Observe the strong discrete DC spike at $f = 0$ Hz and the first spectral null at $R_b = 10\text{ kHz}$.
  4. Select Bipolar NRZ (AMI): Observe that the DC component drops to strictly zero ($-\infty\text{ dBm}$), with the peak power located around $5\text{ kHz}$ ($R_b/2$).
  5. Select Bi-phase Manchester: Observe that the DC component is completely absent ($S(0) = 0$), with the first spectral null shifted out to $2R_b = 20\text{ kHz}$.
  6. Select Unipolar RZ: Observe the discrete line spectrum showing a distinct clock spike at $10\text{ kHz}$, enabling direct clock extraction via a simple tuned tank circuit.

Mathematical Power Spectral Density (PSD) Formulas

For bit rate $R_b = 10\text{ kbps}$, bit duration $T_b = 1/R_b = 100\,\mu\text{s}$, and peak voltage $V$:

Line Code Analytical PSD Formula S(f) DC Component S(0) First Null
Unipolar NRZ $S(f) = \frac{V^2 T_b}{4} \left(\frac{\sin \pi f T_b}{\pi f T_b}\right)^2 + \frac{V^2}{4}\delta(f)$ $\frac{V^2}{4}\delta(0)$ (High DC Spike) $f = R_b = 10\text{ kHz}$
Polar NRZ $S(f) = V^2 T_b \left(\frac{\sin \pi f T_b}{\pi f T_b}\right)^2$ $V^2 T_b$ (Low/Zero DC) $f = R_b = 10\text{ kHz}$
Unipolar RZ $S(f) = \frac{V^2 T_b}{16} \left(\frac{\sin \pi f T_b/2}{\pi f T_b/2}\right)^2 \left[1 + \sum \delta(f - m/T_b)\right]$ Spike at $f=0$ and $f=10\text{ kHz}$ $f = 2R_b = 20\text{ kHz}$
Polar RZ $S(f) = \frac{V^2 T_b}{4} \left(\frac{\sin \pi f T_b/2}{\pi f T_b/2}\right)^2$ Zero if balanced $f = 2R_b = 20\text{ kHz}$
Bipolar NRZ (AMI) $S(f) = V^2 T_b \left(\frac{\sin \pi f T_b}{\pi f T_b}\right)^2 \sin^2(\pi f T_b)$ S(0) = 0 (Zero DC) $f = R_b = 10\text{ kHz}$
Bi-phase Manchester $S(f) = V^2 T_b \left(\frac{\sin \pi f T_b/2}{\pi f T_b/2}\right)^2 \sin^2(\pi f T_b/2)$ S(0) = 0 (Zero DC) $f = 2R_b = 20\text{ kHz}$

Practical Lab Viva Voce Questions & Answers

Q1: Why is DC component elimination important in line coding?
Ans: Communication channels typically contain AC coupling components (transformers, series capacitors). A DC bias causes DC baseline drift, saturates magnetic transformer cores, and wastes transmitter power without transferring information. Line codes like AMI and Manchester guarantee zero DC.
Q2: Why does Bi-phase Manchester require double the bandwidth of NRZ?
Ans: Manchester introduces a mandatory transition at the midpoint of every bit period. Thus, the effective pulse duration is $T_b/2$, pushing the first spectral null out to $2 R_b = 20\text{ kHz}$, compared to $10\text{ kHz}$ for NRZ.
Q3: What is the main advantage of Alternate Mark Inversion (AMI)?
Ans: AMI provides complete DC balance with zero baseline wander, uses the same bandwidth as NRZ (1.0 bps/Hz), and provides intrinsic error detection through AMI Code Violations (consecutive marks of identical polarity).
Q4: What is the limitation of AMI during long runs of zeros?
Ans: During long strings of zeros, the signal stays at 0V with no transitions, causing receiver clock recovery PLLs to lose synchronization. In telecom trunks, zero substitution codes like B8ZS and HDB3 are used to solve this.
